Why we still draw plans by hand first

Every project at the studio still starts the same way it did a decade ago: a pencil, a roll of trace paper, and a plan drawn loosely enough to still be wrong. Not because we're precious about tools, but because software makes a decision feel more finished than it is — and the earliest decisions are exactly the ones that most need to stay unsettled for a while.

A hand sketch is quick to throw away. Redrawing a wall by hand costs thirty seconds; moving it in a finished model can cascade through dimensions, sections, and a dozen linked views. That difference in cost changes behaviour. When changing your mind is cheap, you do it more — and a plan that has survived a dozen cheap revisions tends to be a better plan than one that was locked in on the first attempt because revising it felt expensive.

It also keeps the conversation with a client in the room instead of on a screen. Sketching across the table, in front of someone, makes it obvious in real time when a layout doesn't match how they actually live — something that's much easier to miss when everyone's looking at a polished render instead of a rough line.

None of this is against software. Once a plan has held up under enough scrutiny to earn the details, it moves to CAD and stays there for the rest of the project — drawings, coordination, construction documents, all of it. The hand-drawn stage isn't a style choice; it's just the cheapest way we've found to be wrong quickly, before being wrong gets expensive.
